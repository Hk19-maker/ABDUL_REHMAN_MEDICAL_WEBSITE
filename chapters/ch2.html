Chapter 2

The origins of surgery can be traced back thousands of years. Even before scientific medicine existed, early civilizations attempted to treat injuries and illnesses using the limited knowledge and tools available to them. Ancient Egyptian medical texts describe wound cleaning, bandaging techniques, bone setting, and even basic suturing. Archaeological findings show evidence of trepanation — a procedure in which a hole was made in the skull — likely performed to relieve pressure or treat head injuries. In ancient India, Sushruta, often referred to as the “Father of Surgery,” documented hundreds of surgical procedures and described numerous instruments. His work included early plastic surgery techniques, particularly nasal reconstruction. Chinese and Mesopotamian healers also contributed to early medical practices. Though many treatments were mixed with spiritual beliefs, practical techniques for managing trauma were developed. Despite these advancements, there was no understanding of infection, bacteria, or proper hygiene. Mortality rates were high. Pain control was minimal or nonexistent. However, these early attempts formed the foundation of surgical practice. They marked the beginning of humanity’s effort to repair the body through direct intervention.
